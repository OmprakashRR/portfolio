Summer: limit overheating

Compare the temperature swings. Then add 80% shading and night ventilation. Mass stores heat; shading limits heat entering, while cool night air can remove it.

Winter: retain useful gains

Choose winter and leave shading at 0%. Compare afternoon and late-evening temperatures. Stored solar heat helps later, but mass cannot replace a heat source.

Isolate the effect

Turn ventilation off and hold radiation and shading fixed. Change only heat capacity. Explain the differences in the peak, minimum and time of peak.

Thermal mass is storage; insulation resists heat flow. Both rooms have the same windows and heat-loss coefficient. More mass does not automatically make a room colder in summer or warm enough in winter.

Calculate the same process by hand

Use an energy balance: solar heat + heat from outdoors = change in stored heat.

Name: __________________________   Date: __________________

This fixed exercise starts both rooms at 20°C, with outdoor air at 25°C. Calculate the next 10 minutes. Keep the starting heat-flow rates constant during this short step, exactly as the simulation does.

Psolar = I × A × g × (1 − s)
Qnet = [Psolar + H(Tout − T)] × Δt / 1,000
ΔT = Qnet / C   ·   Tnext = T + ΔT

P: heat rate (W) · I: solar radiation at the window (W/m²) · A: window area (m²) · g: solar gain factor · s: shaded fraction · H: heat-loss coefficient (W/°C) · Δt: time (s) · Q: energy (kJ) · C: effective heat capacity (kJ/°C)

  1. Repeat with 80% shading. Which term changes?
  2. At night, I = 0. If indoors is 20°C and outdoors is 15°C, find ΔT over 10 minutes for both rooms.
  3. Why does higher thermal mass slow both warming and cooling?

C describes the energy needed to raise the whole effective room system by 1°C. For its participating materials, C ≈ Σ(mc). This exercise is a standard snapshot, not the repeating-day graph’s noon state.

Worked answer · Same heat input, different response

Psolar = 400 × 3 × 0.60 × 1 = 720 W
Poutdoor = 100 × (25 − 20) = 500 W into the room
Qnet = (720 + 500) × 600 / 1,000 = 732 kJ

High mass

ΔT = 732 / 6,000 = 0.122°C
Tnext = 20.122°C

Low mass

ΔT = 732 / 1,000 = 0.732°C
Tnext = 20.732°C

With 80% shading: solar gain = 144 W; Qnet = 386.4 kJ. High-mass rise = 0.0644°C; low-mass rise = 0.3864°C.

At night, without extra ventilation: solar gain = 0 W; outdoor exchange = −500 W; Qnet = −300 kJ. High mass cools by 0.050°C; low mass cools by 0.300°C.

Why: six times the heat capacity gives one-sixth of the temperature change for the same net energy. Mass does not create heat or make heat disappear.

Connect the concepts

Radiation, storage and room temperature

What happens to sunlight?

Short-wave solar radiation passes through glazing and is absorbed by internal surfaces. This becomes stored thermal energy. Warmer surfaces then exchange heat with room air by convection and with other surfaces by long-wave radiation.

Here, the solar gain factor includes the net glazing effect. Internal exchanges are combined into one room heat balance, rather than modelling every surface separately.

Does a 62°C material make the room 62°C?

No. A local material or surface temperature is not the room temperature. In Q = mcΔT, the result applies to the material represented by m and c. A rise of 62.5°C from 20°C means 82.5°C for that material under the assumed storage conditions.

This simplified tool uses a combined room temperature. It cannot predict a sunlit surface reaching 62°C or the temperature of individual walls.

Model assumptions, equations and sources
  • One lumped temperature represents each room’s air, thermally active fabric and contents. The high-mass capacity defaults to 6,000 kJ/°C and the low-mass capacity to 1,000 kJ/°C. These illustrative effective capacities are not specifications for a particular construction.
  • Baseline heat-loss coefficient H = 100 W/°C combines fabric transmission and background ventilation. Identical windows: A = 3 m² and g = 0.60. Only capacity differs in the basic comparison.
  • Summer: outdoor temperature 22 + 7 sin[2π(t − 9)/24] °C; daylight 06:00–20:00. Winter: 5 + 4 sin[2π(t − 9)/24] °C; daylight 08:00–16:00. Window-plane solar radiation follows a half-sine during daylight. Default peak: 600 W/m² in summer; 350 W/m² in winter. This is illustrative weather, not a measured location.
  • Shading multiplies solar gains by (1 − s). Optional night ventilation adds 150 W/°C from 22:00 to 06:00, only when outdoor air is cooler than the model room. Both rooms use the same control rule.
  • Each 600-second step uses Tnext = T + [IAg(1 − s) + H(Tout − T)] × 600 / (1,000 C). The temperatures used in the heat-flow calculation are those at the start of the step. The hand-check button calls this same update function.
  • The chart shows a repeating daily cycle, calculated until successive day-start temperatures differ by less than 0.0000001°C. No thermostat, heating, cooling or occupancy gains are included. A cold winter room is expected without sufficient heat input.
  • Mass reduces and delays temperature changes in the basic comparison; it does not itself remove heat. Without night ventilation, adding mass does not reduce the repeating cycle’s mean temperature in this linear model. Insulation, shading and cool-air ventilation serve different purposes.
  • The model does not resolve heat conduction through wall layers, surface temperatures, thermal bridges, moisture, solar geometry, operative temperature or radiant comfort. Only the effective mass that participates in the daily cycle belongs in C.
