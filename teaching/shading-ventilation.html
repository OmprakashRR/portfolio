1. Follow the shadow

Hold the sun at 45°. Change the overhang from 0 to 1.5 m. Then compare high and low sun. Why can the same overhang admit more winter sunlight?

2. Test the breeze

At 22°C outdoors, compare closed and open vents. Set wind to zero. Does opening a vent guarantee airflow in this wind-only model?

3. Challenge the rule

Choose hot outdoor air. Does more ventilation initially cool the 28°C room? Distinguish cooling the air from a breeze making a person feel cooler.

Shading prevents solar gain; ventilation exchanges heat with outdoors. External louvres reduce solar gain when they block more sun. Incoming air removes sensible heat only while it is cooler than the room. This model does not calculate humidity or the comfort benefit of air movement.

A 75 m³ room starts at 28°C; outdoor air is 22°C. Its window is 2 m wide × 1.5 m high. A horizontal overhang projects 0.75 m directly from the window head. The sun altitude is 45°.

Given quantityValue
Direct / diffuse radiation on the vertical window plane500 / 100 W/m²
Window area A; height h; solar gain factor g3 m²; 1.5 m; 0.60
Louvre solar transmission τ (20% blocked)0.80
Maximum controlling opening area A₀; open fraction f0.40 m²; 0.50
Wind effectiveness Cw; wind speed v0.50; 2 m/s
Air density ρ; specific heat cp1.2 kg/m³; 1,000 J/(kg·°C)
Other heat-loss coefficient H₀; room heat capacity C60 W/°C; 3,000 kJ/°C
Time step Δt600 seconds = 10 minutes

A. Find the solar heat gain

Shadow height y = D tan α
Shaded fraction F = min(1, y/h)
Psolar = gτ[IdirectA(1 − F) + IdiffuseA]
  1. Calculate shadow height y and shaded fraction F.
  2. Find the area still receiving direct sunlight.
  3. Calculate solar heat entering the room, in W.

Use degrees on your calculator. The overhang blocks direct sun only in this simplified model; louvres reduce both components.

B. Find the ventilation cooling

V̇ = Cw × (A₀f) × v
ACH = V̇ × 3,600 / room volume
Pvent,out = ρcpV̇(T − Tout)
  1. Find opening area A₀f and airflow V̇ in m³/s.
  2. Convert to m³/h and air changes per hour (ACH).
  3. Find the ventilation heat removal at the start, in W.

C. Combine the gains and losses

Pother,out = H₀(T − Tout)
Qnet = (Psolar − Pvent,out − Pother,out) × Δt / 1,000
ΔT = Qnet/C   ·   Tnext = T + ΔT
  1. Calculate the other heat losses and the net energy change, in kJ.
  2. Calculate the temperature after the first 10 minutes.
  3. Repeat the ventilation heat-flow calculation for 34°C outdoors. Explain its sign.

For this step, hold heat-flow rates at their starting values. The simulation repeats this calculation every 10 minutes. C includes the room’s thermally active fabric and contents; it is an assumed teaching value, not just the heat capacity of the air.

A. Solar heat gain

y = 0.75 × tan(45°) = 0.75 m
F = 0.75 / 1.5 = 0.50 = 50%
Sunlit area = 3 × (1 − 0.50) = 1.50 m²
Psolar = 0.60 × 0.80 × [(500 × 1.50) + (100 × 3)]
Psolar = 0.48 × 1,050 = 504 W

B. Airflow and cooling

Aopening = 0.40 × 0.50 = 0.20 m²
V̇ = 0.50 × 0.20 × 2 = 0.20 m³/s = 720 m³/h
ACH = 720 / 75 = 9.6 h⁻¹
Pvent,out = 1.2 × 1,000 × 0.20 × (28 − 22) = 1,440 W removed

C. Temperature change

Pother,out = 60 × (28 − 22) = 360 W removed
Pnet = 504 − 1,440 − 360 = −1,296 W
Qnet = −1,296 × 600 / 1,000 = −777.6 kJ
ΔT = −777.6 / 3,000 = −0.2592°C
Tnext = 28 − 0.2592 = 27.7408°C

Hot-air challenge

At 34°C outdoors, Pvent,out = 1.2 × 1,000 × 0.20 × (28 − 34) = −1,440 W. Negative removal means 1,440 W enters the room. The breeze initially warms the room air.

Assumptions, limitations and sources
  • The overhang is directly above the glazing and extends far enough sideways to neglect edge effects. The sun lies in a vertical plane perpendicular to the façade. Shadow height is D tan α, capped at the 1.5 m window height. There is no gap above the window.
  • Direct and diffuse irradiance are specified on the vertical window plane; do not multiply them by sin α or cos α again. Presets use illustrative irradiance and temperatures, not a measured location or exact seasonal forecast.
  • Overhang shading reduces the direct component only. Diffuse/reflected-sky shading is neglected. The external-louvre control is an idealised solar blocking fraction, not a prediction from a physical blade angle. Its screen sketch is illustrative. Real closed louvres can still transmit heat and alter airflow.
  • Glazing is fixed; separate opposing vents provide ventilation. This avoids changing the glazing’s solar properties when the vents open. Louvre shading and vent opening are independently controlled.
  • Wind-only cross ventilation: V̇ = Cw Aopening v, with Cw = 0.50, Aopening = 0.40 × open fraction m², and an equal or larger outlet assumed. The opening area is not doubled for the two vents; the coefficient represents the whole flow path. Real flows depend on pressures, obstructions, orientation and opening geometry. Buoyancy/stack flow is omitted, so zero wind gives zero additional ventilation here.
  • Room volume is 75 m³; effective heat capacity is 3,000 kJ/°C. A single temperature represents room air and thermally active fabric/contents. H₀ = 60 W/°C represents other transmission and background losses. Air properties are rounded: ρ = 1.2 kg/m³ and cp = 1,000 J/(kg·°C).
  • The simulation uses Tnext = T + [Psolar − (H₀ + ρcpV̇)(T − Tout)] × 600 / (1,000 C). The 10-minute update is a teaching approximation; shorter steps improve precision, especially at high airflow. Each run starts at 28°C. Solar conditions and outdoor temperature remain constant for six hours so students can isolate each effect. The baseline has no overhang, no louvre shading and closed vents under the same weather.
  • Opening a vent does not instantly set the room temperature to outdoors. Ventilation alone cannot cool this model below the incoming air temperature. Air-movement comfort, humidity, HVAC, occupancy, opaque-wall solar gains and individual surface temperatures are not simulated.
  • Shading prevents gains; ventilation changes heat exchange; insulation changes resistance; thermal mass changes heat-storage capacity. These are separate mechanisms.

Sources: Your Home — shading principles; EnergyPlus — wind-driven ventilation equation, section 1.17.5; US DOE — natural ventilation and cooling.
